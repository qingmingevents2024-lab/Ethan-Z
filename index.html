Getting started

What is a Violin?

The violin is a four-stringed instrument, played with a bow, that sits under your chin. It's the smallest and highest-pitched member of the string family — below it come the viola, cello, and double bass.

Its four strings are tuned to G, D, A, and E. Players change pitch by pressing the strings against the fingerboard with their left hand while drawing the bow across the strings with their right.

Parts of a Violin

  • Pegs: turn these to tune the strings.
  • Nut: holds the strings at the right spacing.
  • Fingerboard: the smooth black board you press to change notes.
  • Body: the wooden part that amplifies the sound.
  • F-holes: openings that help sound project.
  • Bridge: holds the strings up and carries their vibration into the body.
  • Strings: tuned to G, D, A, and E.
  • Tailpiece: anchors the strings at the bottom.
  • Fine tuners: small screws for precise tuning.
  • Chin rest: supports your chin while you play.
  • End button: holds the tailpiece in place.
Reading the language of music

Violin Basics

Dynamics means how loud or soft the music is. Here's the scale, from softest to loudest:

ppp — pianississimo pp — pianissimo p — piano mp — mezzo piano mf — mezzo forte f — forte ff — fortissimo fff — fortississimo

Tempo means speed, measured in BPM (beats per minute) — how fast you'd clap along at a steady pace. It's usually marked in the top-left corner of the music; if it isn't, just pick a comfortable speed and adjust.

TermBPMFeel
Larghissimo<20Very, very slow
Grave20–40Slow and solemn
Lento / Largo40–60Slowly and broadly
Larghetto60–66Rather broadly
Adagio66–76Slow and stately
Andante76–108At a walking pace
Moderato108–120Moderate speed
Allegretto112–124Moderately fast
Allegro120–156Fast and bright
Vivace156–184Lively and fast
Presto168–200Extremely fast
Prestissimo200+Even faster than presto

Rhythm is a regular, repeating pattern of sounds. Note lengths (relative to the BPM):

  • Whole note — 4 beats
  • Half note — 2 beats
  • Quarter note — 1 beat
  • Eighth note — ½ beat
  • Sixteenth note — ¼ beat

Beyond note values, whole styles of rhythm exist too: regular (steady, like marches and pop), syncopated (stress on off-beats, like jazz and funk), free (no fixed beat, in some classical and devotional music), and polyrhythm (layered, conflicting beats, common in African and advanced music).

Where it came from

Violin History

The violin emerged in northern Italy in the early 1500s. Master luthier Andrea Amati of Cremona established the instrument's classic shape, size, and proportions, blending features from Renaissance predecessors like the rebec, fiddle, and lira da braccio.

Ancient Roots

Bowed instruments trace back through ancestors like the Arabic rebab and Asian spiked fiddles.

European Transition

Medieval and Renaissance musicians played three-stringed rebecs and arm-held lira da braccios that paved the way for the modern violin family.

The Amati Standard

Andrea Amati standardized the four-string design in Cremona, building instruments for European nobility, including King Charles IX of France.

Sharing the music

Performances

Violin performances happen year-round in schools, concert halls, churches, and festivals. A performance with one player is a solo; larger groups are called chamber music, duets, quartets, or orchestras.

Want to learn more? Visit Mindful Musicians.

From one young player to another

Beginner Tips

  • 🎯 Practice every day, even for just 10–15 minutes.
  • 🎼 Tune your violin before you start practicing.
  • 🏹 Keep your bow straight.
  • 🐢 Play slowly before trying to play fast.
  • 😄 Have fun, and don't give up!
